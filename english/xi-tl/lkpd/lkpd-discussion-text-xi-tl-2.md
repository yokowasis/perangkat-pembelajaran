## **C. SPEAKING (FGD SIMULATION)**

**Target**: Execute a Focus Group Discussion (FGD) / Panel Discussion simulation adhering to Adab al-Ikhtilaf, Tawasuth, and compassionate communication.

### **FGD Task: "Madrasah Digital & Eco-Policy Blueprint"**

Your group (5–6 members) is assigned to conduct a 12-minute formal panel discussion on the topic:  
**"Should Madrasahs Adopt 100% Paperless Digital Textbooks & Examinations?"**

### **Role Assignment**

- **Member 1 (Moderator)**: Opens session, invites speakers, enforces time, synthesizes consensus.
- **Members 2 & 3 (Proponents - Tech & Efficiency Focus)**: Present environmental benefits (saving trees - Cinta Lingkungan) and learning speed.
- **Members 4 & 5 (Opponents - Equity & Well-being Focus)**: Present screen fatigue, digital disparity, and financial cost.
- **Member 6 (Rapporteur / Notulen)**: Records arguments and drafts the final moderate summary.

### **Required Phrasal Expressions**

- **Moderator**: *"Welcome to our panel discussion. Today we examine... I now invite Panelist 1 to present..."*
- **Proponent**: *"Evidence indicates that transitioning to digital textbooks significantly reduces paper consumption, aligning with Cinta Lingkungan..."*
- **Opponent**: *"While that point is valid, on the other hand, evidence suggests that unequal device access may create socio-economic disparity..."*
- **Hedged Rebuttal**: *"It could be argued that digital screen exposure might potentially impact students' eye health if unmonitored..."*
- **Consensus (Tawasuth)**: *"In conclusion, we propose a blended policy that balances digital learning with physical textbooks..."*

**Assessment Criteria**: Fluency & Pronunciation (25%), Accuracy of Grammar/Hedging (25%), Structural Contribution per Role (25%), Adab al-Ikhtilaf & Compassionate Discourse (25%).

**Target**: Analyze a popular science discussion text, identify evidence-based arguments, and reflect on ecological stewardship (*Tawasuth*).

### **Reading Text**

### **Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs) and Global Food Security: Hope or Hazard?**

As the global human population approaches eight billion, securing a stable food supply while protecting expanding agricultural lands presents one of humanity's greatest scientific challenges. In response, agricultural bioengineers developed Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs)—crops engineered to resist pests, withstand droughts, and yield higher nutritional value. However, the widespread adoption of GMO crops has sparked intense scientific, ethical, and environmental debate worldwide.

Proponents of biotechnology assert that GMO crops offer vital solutions to global hunger and climate change adaptation. Empirical studies indicate that drought-resistant maize and flood-tolerant rice varieties allow farmers in developing nations to sustain crop yields despite extreme weather events. Furthermore, proponents highlight that pest-resistant GMO crops significantly reduce the need for chemical pesticides, thereby mitigating soil contamination and protecting agricultural workers. From a socio-economic perspective, supporters argue that higher crop productivity lowers food prices, making essential nutrition accessible to underprivileged populations.

Conversely, environmental bioethicists and organic farming advocates raise serious concerns regarding ecological stewardship and long-term health risks. Opponents argue that introducing genetically modified genes into open ecosystems may potentially cause unintended gene flow into wild plant species, creating invasive "superweeds" that threaten natural biodiversity. In addition, critics express concern over corporate monopolies, as multinational biotechnology firms patent GMO seeds, potentially trapping smallholder farmers in perpetual financial dependence. Furthermore, skeptics note that long-term human health impacts of consuming genetically modified foods require further longitudinal investigation.

In conclusion, evaluating GMO technology requires adopting the principle of **Tawasuth** (moderation) and **Hikmah** (wisdom). Rather than completely rejecting biotechnology out of fear or blindly embracing it without ecological safeguards, policymakers must implement rigorous, independent biosafety assessments. By balancing scientific innovation with deep respect for natural biodiversity (*Cinta Lingkungan*), society can utilize agricultural science responsibly while preserving the sacred balance of creation.
